# Choosing the offer mechanic

Start from why the sale is happening: stock to clear, cash to raise, a list to reward, new buyers to win. A discount that runs only because the date came round teaches buyers to hold off until the next one.

The mechanics below are grouped by the job they do best. Each one lists where it fits, when to skip it, how the ad should look, what can go wrong, and the number that tells you whether it worked.

## Add value without cutting the price

**Gift with purchase**
- Fits: the brand should not be seen discounting, or you want to reward people who already buy.
- Skip if: buyers would need to read the terms twice, or nobody actually wants the gift.
- The ad: the gift sits in the main image; one short line gives the condition ("spend X, get Y"); a real limit on stock or dates.
- Watch for: gift cost and running out; buyers stopping at the qualifying spend rather than adding more; with no qualifying spend at all it is a discount by another name.
- Read it by: order value and order count against the baseline, the new-customer share, how many gifts were claimed.

**Free-shipping threshold**
- Fits: delivery cost is what stops people checking out, or order value should rise with no price cut.
- Skip if: the level would sit under what most of your orders already reach, so it changes nothing.
- The ad: a site banner, a cart bar that shows how far the buyer has to go, and ad copy that states the level. Pick the level from your own order values: somewhere between the median order and the upper quartile, so a typical basket has a short, reachable gap.
- Watch for: paying delivery on many small orders; a level so low it gives margin away for nothing.
- Read it by: the change in order value, and how many orders land just over the level.

**Bundles**
- Fits: products are used together, a slower line needs to move, or you want a reference price with no headline percentage.
- Skip if: the items have nothing to do with each other.
- The ad: show every item and the sum of their prices beside the bundle price; open on what the set is for, not the saving.
- Watch for: pairing a slow line with a best seller can weigh the best seller down.
- Read it by: how often the bundle is chosen, margin per order, how fast stock turns.

## Grow the basket

**Buy more, save more (tiers)**
- Fits: you need bigger orders more than more visitors; stock runs deep; the deepest tier still leaves margin.
- Skip if: the top tier would barely pay, or the people seeing the ad do not know the brand well enough to buy several things at once.
- The ad: open with the best tier, show the steps as a simple ladder, and tell the buyer in the cart what the next step needs.
- Watch for: margin lost at the top step; returns from people who bought extra; a clever ladder that confuses.
- Read it by: order value, units per order, margin per order, the return rate.

**Buy one, get one (or the second item reduced)**
- Fits: one line has deep stock and needs units moved.
- Skip if: a free or cheap second item wipes out the margin.
- The ad: two items pictured side by side; "the second one" in the opening words.
- Watch for: buyers come to think the item is worth half its price.
- Read it by: units per order, margin per order, how much of that line sold.

## Clear stock

**Red-line markdown**
- Fits: end of a season, with stock that has to go.
- Skip if: the markdown would spill onto lines that still sell at full price.
- The ad: the original price crossed out next to the new one; a named group of products ("final reductions").
- Watch for: buyers learning to wait for the markdown; the original price must be one the product genuinely sold at (`consumer-law.md`).
- Read it by: how much of the marked-down stock sold, not revenue across the store.

**"Take a further X% off"**
- Fits: the last step of a markdown done in stages, to move whatever is left.
- Skip if: the earlier step has not run long enough to show what it does.
- The ad: present it as "now reduced again", followed by a genuine closing window.
- Watch for: margin drops quickly, and by this point the sale audience has seen a lot of ads, so each sale costs more. Plan for a higher cost per sale; do not answer it with more budget.
- Read it by: stock sold, margin after the step, and cost per sale compared with the step before.

## Reach and simplicity

**Sitewide**
- Fits: one simple message and the most volume are worth the margin it costs.
- Skip if: best sellers or the brand's price position need protecting.
- The ad: a single offer, the same on every surface.
- Watch for: the largest margin cost and the strongest habit of waiting; "sitewide" has to cover every product (`consumer-law.md`).
- Read it by: total contribution after costs, not revenue.

**Category or collection**
- Fits: protecting best sellers, letting stock decide the creative, a cleaner read afterwards.
- Skip if: the category is too small to move the total.
- The ad: led by the category; state plainly which lines are left out.
- Watch for: disappointing buyers who came for a line that is excluded.
- Read it by: how much of the category sold, and its contribution.

## Openers, closers and surprises

**Early access**
- Fits: you have subscribers or a loyalty base and want a strong first day you can read cleanly.
- Skip if: the list is too small to matter, or the "early" link spreads publicly so nothing is exclusive.
- The ad: email and SMS go first and ads reach the list again; sell being first, and make being first worth it: more time, or a better perk than the public sale gets.
- Watch for: it pulls sales forward, so the following day can be quieter.
- Read it by: first-day revenue against plan, the share of it from your own channels, the share from returning buyers.

**Sale extended**
- Fits: demand or stock is genuinely left over, or trading time was lost.
- Skip if: it is a habit. An extension every time teaches buyers that no deadline is real.
- The ad: fresh creative (a neighbouring angle, not a repeat), a new end date shorter than the first run, and the reason for the extra days.
- Watch for: extending after copy that said the sale was ending can mislead (`consumer-law.md`).
- Read it by: revenue in the extra days compared with the same days in the baseline.

**Mystery offer**
- Fits: a playful brand whose subscribers read and click what it sends.
- Skip if: you are unsure what the promotion rules are where you sell.
- The ad: say what the odds are and the least anyone will get.
- Watch for: promotion rules differ by place; buyers let down by a small minimum.
- Read it by: redemptions, order value, complaints.

## Common mistakes

Before the sale:
- Running a sale only because it is on the calendar, with no stock, cash or customer reason behind it.
- Leaving the creative brief until the sale is already live.
- Terms a buyer cannot take in at a glance.
- Assuming the offer that worked in one market will work in every other.

During the sale:
- Putting sale ads inside the always-on campaigns rather than their own.
- Using the launch ad again for the middle, the extension and the last day.
- Extending without new creative or a reason.
- Answering a falling return with more budget instead of new creative.

After the sale:
- Leaving sale budgets and targets switched on once it has finished.
- Calling it a success on platform-reported revenue without checking margin, returns and how many buyers were new.
